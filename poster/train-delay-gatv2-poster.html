RAILWAY DELAY FORECASTING · GRAPH NEURAL NETWORKS

Direct Multi-Horizon GATv2 for Train Delay Prediction

Replacing autoregressive rollout with direct multi-horizon decoding in an event-based, topology-aware graph attention framework — testing whether direct prediction curbs horizon-dependent error accumulation.

01

Motivation

Railway operations are a web of dependent events, not isolated observations. A primary delay at one train event can spread to neighboring events through shared infrastructure, dwell interactions, and headway constraints — producing secondary, knock-on delays that cascade through the network.

  • Graph-based learning represents train events as nodes and operational dependencies as edges, letting message passing carry delay information between connected events.
  • Prior work (Nguyen & Li) pairs an event-based GATv2 encoder with autoregressive rollout for multi-step forecasts — but recursively reusing predictions means an early error can compound at later horizons.
  • Research question: is horizon degradation caused by the graph representation itself, or by the autoregressive decoding strategy layered on top of it?
02

Feature engineering

Dwell time Di = tdepi − tarri
Running time Ri = tarri − tdepi−1
Headway Hi = ti − ti−1

Trip context is captured with stop sequence, normalized trip progress, and stops remaining. Time of day is encoded with sine / cosine transforms plus a rush-hour indicator. Data comes from Subway Data NYC observed trips, matched to the official MTA static GTFS schedule by service day.

03

Graph construction

Every arrival and departure is a node (event-based graph). Dwell edges link an arrival to its departure at the same stop; Run edges link a departure to the next arrival on the same trip; Headway edges link consecutive trains sharing route, direction, stop, and event type.

04

Model architecture

A 3-layer GATv2 backbone encodes each event node, feeding a two-stage hurdle (classify delayed / not, then regress magnitude). The proposed Direct Multi-Horizon (DMH) heads predict t+1, t+2, t+3 simultaneously from the current graph state, instead of feeding predictions back in autoregressively. Overlapping horizon estimates for the same event are combined with validation-MAE-based weights.

4.4 pts higher recall for DMH over autoregressive GATv2 (93.8% vs 89.9%), and DMH's MAE stays nearly flat across horizons (2.65 → 2.75 min) while the autoregressive model's error climbs from 2.41 to 3.12 min — the signature of compounding error.

06

Conclusion

Direct multi-horizon decoding preserves the topology-aware strengths of the event-based GATv2 encoder while measurably curbing horizon-dependent error growth. DMH is the stronger delay detector and the more stable regressor at longer horizons, even though the persistence baseline remains hard to beat on raw MAE — a reminder that subway delays are often locally stable, and that model value here lies in decision support rather than beating a naive baseline outright. Future work: sweep the horizon length K, and test on networks with more frequent sudden disruptions and congestion.
